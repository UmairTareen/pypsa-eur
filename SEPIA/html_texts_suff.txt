BE_CO2_emissions_sector: <p> The CO₂ emissions for each sector and planning horizon indicate that:</p> <ul> <li>The majority of emission reductions occur in the early years of the transition, primarily driven by the rapid electrification of transportation and space heating.</li> <li>By 2050, most remaining emissions come from the industrial sector due to the usage of fuels (e.g., for high-temperature processes and chemical indusry) and process-related emissions, such as those from cement clinker calcination.</li> <li>Negative emissions mainly result from biomass-based CO₂ capture. However, this capture declines between 2040 and 2050 due to limitations in biomass availability and sustainability concerns. The CO₂ absorbed by biomass is eventually released back when the biomass is utilized, for example, in industrial applications or producing bio fuels or space heating in the residential and tertiary sectors. </li> <li>Industrial process emissions directly in the atmosphere are almost entirely eliminated by 2040, largely due to post combustion carbon capture applications.</li> <li>No negative emissions from carbon sequestration are present, as sequestration is not permitted in the sufficiency scenario. However, carbon capture and utilization (CCU) is assumed to mitigate or neutralize process emissions without generating net negative emissions, as it is totally utilised to produce synthetic oils and methanol.</li> </ul>


BE_CO2_emissions_source: <p> CO₂ emissions for each source from 2019 to 2050 clearly indicate that the reduction in emissions is closely linked to the decline in petroleum and natural gas imports. In contrast, other sources of emissions, such as those from agriculture, have a lower potential for reduction.</p>

BE_Cumulative_CO2_emissions_source: <p> This plot shows the cumulative CO₂ emissions at the end of transition period and contribution of each source for each year.</p>

BE_sankey_diagram: <p> The sankey diagram illustrates the energy flows and conversion from primary energy sources to final energy demands. It can be displayed for each simulated time period (2019, 2030, 2040, 2050).</p> <p>Some key insights from the Sankey diagrams include:</p><ul><li>Belgium increases its energy independence to 58%, up from about 6% today.</li><li>Renewable electricity is primarily produced locally.</li><li>Biomass consumption slightly increases and is fully utilized up to its maximum potential.</li><li>Biomass is the primary source of methane for the gas grid, produced via biogas from wet biomass and methanation from dry biomass. </li><li>  The gas grid remains but is almost exclusively used for industry. Residential and tertiary distribution gas grids become obsolete and are gradually decommissioned.</li><li>Imports and energy dependency mainly involve green hydrogen, supplied via a pan-European hydrogen backbone.</li><li>This hydrogen is used for non-energy demands, transport and to produce synthetic fuels in the sufficiency scenario.</li><li>Electricity becomes the dominant energy vector, carrying 168 TWh in 2050 compared to 93 TWh in 2019.</li><li>Battery electric vehicles dominate, except for long-distance freight. Their electricity consumption reaches 9 TWh in 2050.</li><li>Low-temperature heat is primarily supplied by heat pumps, with a small contribution from biomass. About 25% is district heating, incorporating thermal energy storage to enable load shifting.</li></ul>District heating is predominantly powered by electric boilers rather than heat pumps, and combined with long-duration thermal energy storage. This preference arises from the significantly lower capital investment required for large-scale heat pumps compared to electric boilers, allowing to charge the storage during the times of excess renewable generation.

BE_carbon_sankey_diagram: <p> The carbon sankey diagram illustrates the carbon flow (expressed in tons of CO2) for each source and sector and can be displayed for each simulated time period (2019, 2030, 2040, 2050).</p><p>Although the simulated region (Belgium, France, Germany, Netherlands, UK) achieves full carbon neutrality by 2050, Belgium is allowed a residual net emission of 5% compared to 1990 levels. Due to the limited carbon sink potential of the Belgian territory (LULUCF), it is more practical to allocate these residual emissions to neighboring countries with greater capacity.</p> <p>The carbon Sankey also reveals that:</p><ul><li>All process emissions are captured and utilized to produce e-fuels (synthetic oil and methanol), which are later re-emitted.</li><li>A significant and challenging source of carbon emissions comes from the agriculture sector, which is expected to reduce its emissions from 10 Mtons in 2019 to 5 Mtons in 2050.</li><li>Some emissions are embedded in the import and export of synthetic fuels: imported methanol contributes to positive emissions, while exported bio-sourced natural gas accounts for negative emissions.</li></ul>

BE_sectoral_demands_desc: <p>This figure displays the final energy and non-energy demands for each carrier across different sectors.</p> <p>Due to efficiency improvements in final energy conversion technologies and the sufficiency assumptions, it is evident that many of these demands decrease over time:</p><ul><li>Fossil fuel-based feedstocks for industry are reduced and gradually replaced by hydrogen starting in 2040.</li><li> Low temperature space heating declines due to deep renovations and improved building energy efficiency. By 2050, district heating covers 25% of the demand.</li><li>Final electricity demand rises, driven by the electrification of transportation and industrial processes.</li><li>Oil demand sharply decreases, with a residual 4 TWh for aviation in 2050 and 3 TWh for long-haul freight.</li><li>Gas use in industry also decreases, with a portion replaced by electricity.</li><li>Hydrogen is consumed for production of feedstocks, ammonia and steel in indutrial sectors, and for long-distance transport. Its share in final energy demand remains relatively low, confirming its main role as an intermediate (secondary) energy carrier.</li><li>Solid biomass remains relatively stable and is used for high-temperature heat in industry.</li></ul>

BE_annual_costs_desc: <p>The total annual costs include investment (CAPEX) and operational (OPEX) expenses. While CAPEX slightly increases, OPEX is significantly reduced, mainly because of the decreased importations of fossil fuels.</p> 

BE_annual_clustered_costs_desc: <p>The total annual costs include investment (CAPEX) and operational (OPEX) expenses. "Uses" refer to the investment in non-electricity producing technologies such as power-to-heat or power-to-gas technologies.</p> 

BE_investment_costs_desc: <p>The annual investment costs are annualized and don't necessarily represent the invesments to be realized in the given time period. This appears clearly for the nuclear techology: annualized invesment cost are displayed for 2019 and 2030 while no investment in new nuclear units is foreseen in the scenario. </p><p>The following technologies also deserve specific attention:</p><ul><li>Biogas and BioSNG (methanation of dry biomass) are deployed as early as 2030, and gain significance by 2040.</li><li>Substantial investments in heat pumps are required throughout the entire energy transition.</li><li>Investment in other power-to-X (e.g. power-to-gas) technologies steadily increases from 2030 to 2050.</li><li>The investment in grids and interconnections (both AC and DC) reaches one billion Euros per year, higher than the annual investment needed for distribution grids.</li><li>Major investments are necessary for solar and wind power generation technologies, reaching one third of the total required investment cost by 2050.</li></ul>

BE_operational_costs_desc: <p>The annual operational costs show all the operational costs which include fuel costs and also variable operational and maintenence costs of technologies. In the Negawatt-BE scenario, it is assumed that a hydrogen backbone is built over the whole EU, and that Belgium can import green hydrogen in large quantities. This green hydrogen becomes the main operational cost by 2050.</p>

BE_carbon_elec_costs_desc: <p>This plot shows average electricity price and carbon price for each planning horizon. Average electricity prices decrease because of the higher penetration of zero-marginal cost power generation. CO2 prices increase sitnificantly and are then reduced by 2050 because of the assumed progress of energy sufficiency measures, which decrease the consumption.</p>


BE_capacities_desc: <p>The evolution of installed capacities for several key technologies is illustrated below.</p><ul><li>Solar deployment includes both rooftop and ground-mounted installations, reaching 14 GWp by 2030 and 48 GWp by 2050—still below the country's maximum potential.</p></li><li>Offshore wind achieves its full potential of 8 GW by 2040. Notably, this scenario does not consider the additional offshore capacity proposed in the Ostend Declaration.</p></li><li>By 2050, AC and DC grid interconnections with neighboring countries more than double, providing greater flexibility for integrating variable renewable energy sources.</p></li><li>Gas power plants (CCGT) are used to provide flexibility and meet the increased demand from electrification. These plants serve as a transitional solution and are later replaced by further renewable energy deployment. Nuclear power is phased out according to the decommissioning plan for Doel and Tihange: by 2030, only Doel 4 and Tihange 3 remain operational. By 2040, no nuclear units are left in the country, and the model does not select new investments due to their prohibitive costs.</p>

BE_storage_capacities_desc: <p>The storage capacitiy strongly varies depending on the energy carrier.</p> <ul><li>The gas storage capacity remains constant at 7.6 TWh, corresponding to the Loenhout storage facility. It is the only technology enabling seasonal storage in the country. Although the model allows its conversion into hydrogen storage (with lower capacity), it remains dedicated to natural gas throughout the transition, with no significant investments in hydrogen storage.</li><li>Battery storage emerges as early as 2030 and reaches more than 80 GWh storage capacity in 2040 to support grid balancing.</li><li>Thermal energy storage is introduced alongside the expansion of district heating networks, providing additional grid flexibility through load shifting, it is fully deployed by 2040.

BE_heat_dispatch_sum_desc: <p>The heat dispatch charts illustrate the heat production and distribution during a typical summer week.</p>

BE_power_dispatch_win_desc: <p>The power dispatch charts show electricity production and distribution during a typical winter week.</p>

BE_power_dispatch_sum_desc: <p>The power dispatch charts show electricity production and distribution during a typical summer week.</p>

BE_map_plots_desc: <p>The map below provides a geographical overview of the investments in energy infrastructure and associated electricity grid expansion. 35% expansion of interconnections were allowed for each planning horizon considering the time required for planning, approval and construction of HV transmission line projects.<p></p> Important insights from results for Belgium include:</p><ul><li> Considering the Belgium GDP in 2023 (568 billion Euros), in the sufficiency scenario, the total investment requirements are 1.3% of the GDP in 2030, 1.8% in 2040, and 2% in 2050. These evestments are further disaggregated in the "costs" sections of this page.</li><li>The ALEGrO HVDC transmission line, connecting Belgium and Germany, and the NemoLink HVDC transmission line, connecting Great Britain and Belgium, both with a capacity of 1 GW in 2020, with each increasing to 2.5 GW by 2050.</li><li> The HVAC transmission lines between Belgium and France are extended to 7 GW by 2050.</li><li> The HVAC transmission lines between Belgium and Netherlands are extended to 8.3 GW by 2050.

BE_h2_map_plots_desc: <p>By 2050, the hydrogen grid is significantly developed in Belgium and in its neightbouring countries, with a preference for converting existing gas pipelines into hydrogen pipelines over investing in new ones. For Belgium, the primary gas pipelines connecting Belgium to Great Britain and France are mostly transformed into hydrogen pipelines by 2050.</p>  

BE_gas_map_plots_desc: <p>The current gas grid undergoes a significant transformation, and by 2050, biogas becomes the primary gas source for Belgium, eliminating the need for fossil gas imports.</p></p>By 2050, the majority of existing gas pipelines connecting Belgium are retrofitted to transport hydrogen.</p>
